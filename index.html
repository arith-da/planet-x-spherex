The sky changes. Here you can see it with your own eyes.

This tool shows real photographs of the same place in the sky taken in different years and compares them right in front of you: blink between them, and whatever moved, appeared, or disappeared jumps out. That is how distant planets like the hypothetical Planet X are hunted in data from NASA's SPHEREx mission.

How the comparison works, in 20 seconds

It is the method Clyde Tombaugh used to discover Pluto in 1930, and it is still the most direct way to hunt for objects that move.

Stars are so far away that over decades they move less than one pixel: they sit in the same place in both photos. Anything that really does move — an asteroid, a nearby fast star, or a distant planet — shows up in a different place in the second photo. When you alternate the photos (blink), that object seems to jump. That jump is the signal we are looking for.

What you are looking for (and why almost nothing moves)

Most stars are so far away that, between the 1950s and the 1990s, they moved less than one pixel in these photos: that is why, when you blink, almost everything looks still — and that is normal. Blinking only betrays what truly changed position or brightness:

  • A nearby, fast star, like Barnard's: it moves 10.3 arcseconds per year, about 5 arcminutes between these epochs. When you blink, it jumps. Press "Mark Barnard" and two rings appear on the photo: the lower ring is exactly where Barnard sits in the 1950s photo and the upper ring where it sits in the 1980s–90s photo (positions measured on these same photos) — blink and watch it jump from ring to ring.
  • An asteroid that happened to cross the field when one of the photos was taken.
  • Something that appeared or disappeared: in Difference mode everything identical turns black and only what changed stays lit; a moved star shows up as two dots.
  • A distant planet: SPHEREx photographs the whole sky every ~6 months, and a planet hundreds of AU away would shift ~1.5–5 arcminutes between maps. This comparator trains your eye on real historical archives.

Honest notice: the 2MASS (1997–2001) and WISE (2010) epochs are temporarily down inside SkyView: its server cannot connect to those remote archives because of a security-certificate problem on NASA's side, and they return an error page instead of a photo. That is why this version compares DSS and DSS2, which do respond today (verified on November 14, 2026). When NASA fixes it, 2MASS and WISE will return as epochs 4 and 5.

Advanced search — search SPHEREx (IRSA) without the headache

You only need one point in the sky. Below you will find a short guide to entering coordinates, a converter if yours are in hours, and the buttons to view the point in the comparator and to query SPHEREx at IRSA. IRSA's official table opens in a new tab: that is the most reliable route while their server fails from time to time. Important: the IRSA table is a list of data files, not pictures — to see images, use the comparator and the sky viewer on this page.

How do you enter coordinates?

A place in the sky is located with two numbers, like latitude and longitude on Earth:

  • RA (Right Ascension): from 0 to 360. It is the left-to-right position. Example: Cetus = 37.5
  • Dec (Declination): from −90 to +90. Positive = north, negative = south. Example: Cetus = −15
  • Field: how much sky you want to see. 0.5 = detail, 2 = wide area.

Ready-to-copy examples:

  • Cetus (the team's region): RA 37.5 · Dec −15
  • Barnard's Star: RA 269.452 · Dec +4.693
  • Pleiades: RA 56.8 · Dec +24.1

Got them in hours? Convert them here

In many books RA is written like 02h 30m 00s. Type it here and press convert: 02h 30m = 37.5°.

Formula: RA° = (h + m/60 + s/3600) × 15.

If IRSA shows "502 Proxy Error": it is not your fault and not this page's. Verified on November 15, 2026: IRSA's query server fails intermittently — the error arrives in under 1 second, while a good answer takes about 20 seconds. What to do: close that tab, wait 1–2 minutes, and press the button again. Keep the radius at 0.05.

Alternatives if IRSA keeps failing: 1) Keep exploring right here with the comparator and the Aladin Lite viewer, which do not depend on that server. 2) Go through IRSA's front door: the SPHEREx archive at IRSA and its official data-access guide, which explains other routes (Firefly, TAP, and cloud access). 3) Check IRSA's cloud access (AWS) from their official site: it is for downloading files by folder, not for searching by coordinates, which is why it is linked out instead of browsed here.
What do you get at IRSA? A table where each row is a SPHEREx file that covers your point. The access_url column is the link to the FITS file. FITS is not a normal photo: it is the professional format holding the image plus its measurements, and the browser does not display it as a picture — that is why here you see the sky in the comparator with SkyView photos and in the Aladin viewer, and at IRSA you download the scientific data.

All-sky viewer — Aladin Lite, inside the page

This is the other place where you see the sky inside this page: an interactive map of the real sky — drag to move around and use the wheel or your fingers to zoom. It is built by the CDS — Strasbourg Astronomical Data Center (France); it is not NASA's, although much of the data it shows does come from NASA missions (DSS, 2MASS, WISE) served from the CDS. Aladin Lite does not include SPHEREx: for SPHEREx, use the IRSA query above. The viewer automatically follows the region you pick in the search tools. Purpose: hunting Planet X with SPHEREx

The SPHEREx mission

SPHEREx is a NASA MIDEX mission launched on March 11, 2025. It is the first spectral survey of the whole sky in the near infrared: it observes in 102 bands between 0.75 and 5.0 µm and will build 4 complete maps of the sky in 2 years, one every ~6 months. Its data are published at IRSA (IPAC, Caltech); the first release, DR1, came out on November 12, 2026. Sources: NASA — SPHEREx mission and the SPHEREx archive at IRSA.

The Planet X (Planet Nine) hypothesis

Batygin and Brown proposed in 2016 that the clustered orbits of distant Kuiper Belt objects are explained by a planet nobody has seen yet: about 4–10 Earth masses, at 300–800 AU from the Sun. As of November 2026 it has not been discovered, not been ruled out, and there is no consensus. The team chose as its initial search region RA 02h 30m (37.5°), Dec −15°, in Cetus, inside the refined 600–800 square-degree area that comes out of the Cassini data (Holman & Payne) and the Batygin & Brown models — but this tool can look at any region of the sky.

The honest caveat

A cold Planet Nine (28–53 K) would emit its thermal peak between 50 and 100 µm, outside SPHEREx's range (up to 5.0 µm). SPHEREx helps another way: its repeated coverage of the whole sky. Such a planet would move about 1.5–5 arcminutes between maps taken 6 months apart, and that is exactly what a blink comparator can betray. That is why every find here is recorded as a candidate that needs follow-up, never as a confirmed detection.

A real candidate from the literature: Phan et al. 2025

Phan and collaborators (2025) found an object that appears in IRAS data (1983) and AKARI data (2006), separated by 47.46 arcminutes in 23 years. It is unconfirmed, and its implied orbital inclination (~120°) does not fit Batygin–Brown's Planet Nine (15–20°). We preload it in the candidate list as an example of how a real case is documented.
